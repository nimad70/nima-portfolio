## The problem

Retrieval-augmented generation is usually framed as a fix for hallucination:
ground the model in retrieved documents and it stops inventing things. That
framing quietly assumes the retrieval corpus is trustworthy.

It often is not. A vector database is frequently the least guarded component of
the stack — populated by an ingestion pipeline, fed from sources that change,
and rarely audited after the fact. If an attacker can influence what goes in,
they influence what the model says, without touching the model at all.

This was my M.Sc. thesis at the University of Padua, carried out during a
research internship at ICIS, Radboud University.

## Approach

I built a full RAG pipeline so the attack could be measured against a working
baseline rather than a toy:

- Document ingestion and chunking over a corpus of PDFs
- Embeddings via InstructorEmbedding, stored in ChromaDB
- Semantic retrieval feeding a LLaMA-2 generator, orchestrated with LangChain

Then I poisoned the store — injecting crafted passages designed to be retrieved
for target queries and to carry misleading content once retrieved — and
measured the change in answer quality against the clean baseline.

Running LLaMA-2 on available hardware meant memory-efficient fine-tuning:
LoRA and QLoRA adapters with 8-bit and 4-bit quantisation, which brought
training within reach of a single consumer GPU.

## Results

Answer quality dropped by **up to 40%** on targeted queries. The failure mode
matters more than the number: the system did not error or refuse. It answered
fluently and confidently, citing retrieved context, and was wrong. Every
surface signal a user relies on to judge trustworthiness stayed intact.

That is the uncomfortable finding. Grounding makes a model *more* persuasive,
so corrupting the grounding is more dangerous than corrupting the model.

## What I would do differently

The evaluation leaned on a narrow set of target queries. A broader sweep,
including queries the poisoning was not tuned for, would say more about how
far the degradation generalises. I would also now measure retrieval precision
separately from answer quality, to separate "the wrong document was retrieved"
from "the right document was retrieved and the model still went wrong".
